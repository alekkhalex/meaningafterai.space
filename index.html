Meaning After Work

A working hypothesis about where each person finds energy when machines do the work

Summary

The people building artificial intelligence, and those who study its consequences, keep returning to one question: when machines can do almost all work, what will people live for? They disagree about how hard it is. Elon Musk thinks meaning will be the real question; Dario Amodei thinks the economics will be harder. Income can at least be redistributed. Work gave people more than income, and no one has yet said what replaces the rest for a particular person.

I propose a working hypothesis that answers at the level of the individual. Everyone has what I call a purpose, in a narrower sense than usual: not a goal or a mission, but a leading trait of character that a person has been practising since childhood. Using it gives people energy and the sense that they are living their own life; being unable to use it drains them. There are people and environments in which a particular person’s purpose fails again and again; I call them radioactive for that person. Living on your own wavelength means knowing your purpose, choosing environments where it works, and leaving those where it fades.

Work was only one such environment, and for many people not a good one. In a world without work, people could for the first time choose their occupations and company according to their purpose. If the hypothesis holds, this can be taught, and it can be tested.

1The problem

It was predicted almost a century ago

In 1930 John Maynard Keynes wrote that within a hundred years the economic problem would largely be solved, and that man would then meet what he called his permanent problem: “how to occupy the leisure, which science and compound interest will have won for him”.1 He warned that people had been shaped for ages by the struggle for subsistence, and that a world without it would leave them without their traditional purpose. His hundred years run out in 2030.

Today it is raised by technologists and scholars alike

Elon Musk says that probably nobody will have a job, and then “the question will really be one of meaning”.2 Dario Amodei, the head of Anthropic, devotes the last section of his essay on a future with AI to work and meaning; he expects people to keep finding meaning in relationships and in tasks they care about, and considers the economic side harder.3 The philosopher Nick Bostrom devoted an entire book to a “solved world” in which human effort is no longer needed for any practical purpose.4 The historian Yuval Noah Harari warns of a class of people whose labour is economically unnecessary.5 The economist Daniel Susskind argues that work has become a central source of meaning and identity for many people, and that a world with too little of it will need deliberate answers.6

Money is not enough

Studying the unemployed of the Austrian village of Marienthal in the 1930s, the psychologist Marie Jahoda saw that work gives people more than income. She later summed it up as five “latent functions”: a structure to the day, contact outside the family, a part in a collective purpose, status and identity, and regular activity. Later research found that retirees, too, often report fewer of these functions than people in work, although they have an income.7 Meta-analyses confirm that unemployment noticeably harms mental health, and that it is generally worse for the long-term unemployed.8

Basic income trials point the same way. Finland’s two-year experiment found recipients more satisfied with life and less strained;9 in a three-year American study, an early drop in stress faded after the first year.10 Neither trial set out to measure meaning, but neither suggests that money alone gives people something to do or a reason to do it.

Free time is not the answer

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi found a paradox: people want more leisure, yet they experience complete absorption more often at work than at rest.11 Viktor Frankl described the “Sunday neurosis”: the dejection that sets in when the busy week ends and an inner emptiness becomes apparent.12 Take work away from millions of people and Sunday becomes permanent.

The proposed answers work at the level of society

A culture of leisure, games, relationships, creative work, a state “leisure policy”.13 These answers are reasonable for society as a whole, but they give no way to match a particular person with what will sustain them. Games energise one person and drain another. One needs people around, another needs solitary inquiry. One flourishes when asked for help, another when left alone.

The question of what to live for once work is no longer needed has no general answer. But every person has their own answer, and it can be found.

2The model: where a person’s energy comes from

The model grew out of my years of work with people: case-by-case work on their relationships, one-to-one consultations (I have recorded more than 150 of them) and regular open meetings. I do not consider it a proven theory. It is a working hypothesis, and in section 6 I propose how to test it.

The starting assumption: happiness

I start from an assumption rather than a finding: everything a person does is aimed at being happy. Most people, I believe, knew this state in early childhood: joy without conditions, simply because you exist. Growing up, a person begins to separate themselves from the world, the world begins to seem threatening, and that state fades. From then on, life becomes a search for the way back, and everyone’s way is their own.

The childhood scene

My hypothesis is that each person’s way is set early, usually between three and seven, in the conflicts a child witnesses between the adults closest to them. Conflict between adults is inevitable: they are two personalities with different goals. But the child experiences the rift between them as a rift inside themselves, and does two things:

  • notices how one of the adults disrupts the relationship: shouting, withdrawing, pressuring, belittling. This becomes their trigger;
  • invents how the other adult should have fixed it: by shouting louder, waiting it out in silence, talking it through, embracing, restoring order, leaving with an ultimatum. This becomes their purpose.

I cannot observe that scene directly. What I work with is the scene as the adult tells it now, and it is this story, accurate or not, that I use to predict where the person gains and loses energy. The model does not deny heredity or temperament; it describes what a child builds on top of them.

I call this pair a relationship program: trigger plus purpose. The child does not remember it as a decision, but goes on practising the invented method for the rest of their life: in kindergarten, at school, with friends, at work. By adulthood they do it better than most people. This is usually called talent or character.

An example. One parent had outbursts of anger that came without warning. The child could not prepare for them and learned to read them in advance: from the voice, the look, small details. This grows into acute empathy and intuition, a real strength that the adult applies in work with people. Alice Miller described the same route to empathy in The Drama of the Gifted Child.14

Only the motivation seems to be innate: pleasure in a certain kind of action. The skill itself is trained. That is why children in the same family, witnessing the same conflict, take different things away from it.

Energy

Every time a person successfully applies their purpose, it is as if the childhood story were replayed with a better ending. The person feels a surge of strength, joy and clarity: “I am living my own life”. When the purpose fails again and again, a decline follows: irritation, fatigue, a sense of meaninglessness, up to prolonged low mood.

I use the word “energy” as a convenient name for observable states: vigour, motivation, mood. Psychologists measure something close to it as subjective vitality.15 It cannot be seen under a microscope, but it is easy to notice in oneself: in your mood after a conversation, in an involuntary smile, in whether you want to return to the task tomorrow.

What charges a person is usually one specific feature of a situation; the rest is backdrop. A field doctor, an animal tamer and an auditor do very different things, but a person whose purpose is “to bring order where there is chaos” gets the same charge from all three. So an occupation should be chosen by that feature; the job title says little.

Radioactive people and environments

If purpose is a way to defuse the trigger, there must be situations where the trigger is stronger than the purpose can handle. These are people and environments that, by their very make-up, do not let a person’s purpose work. I call them radioactive for that person.

Why not “toxic”? Toxicity sounds like an absolute property: the person is bad for everyone. What I mean by radioactivity is invisible and depends on the pair: the same person may be harmless or even pleasant for someone else. Two consequences follow. The mismatch itself is nobody’s fault. And you yourself are radioactive for someone, because a purpose that inspires some people drains others.

How to recognise a radioactive situation:

  • after a meeting the person “stays in your head” for several days, whereas after an ordinary unpleasant conversation you recover within minutes;
  • irritation arises, a sense of “nobody hears me”, “it’s all in vain”;
  • day after day in this environment you feel you are living someone else’s life.

The shape is not new. Yerkes and Dodson described an inverted U between arousal and performance more than a century ago,16 and Seery and colleagues found that people with some adversity in their lives fare better than those with none or with a great deal.17 What the model adds is a hypothesis about which difficulty counts for a given person.

Two phases

Here the model takes a less obvious step. In the first half of life, people are drawn, without knowing it, towards their trigger: difficult people and circumstances serve as a gym where the purpose is trained. Hence a familiar pattern, which psychoanalysis has long described as repetition: a strong attraction to someone who causes pain, and boredom next to a “right”, calm partner.

Once the skill is trained, a turning point comes, which I call the shift. Pain from the trigger stops producing growth and becomes simply pain. A person can leave the gym and start choosing environments where their purpose no longer has to fight, but works and is valued.

A limit. None of this applies to violence, coercion or abuse. There the question is safety, not fit: get out and get help, whatever the sign of readiness says.

Not a “comfort zone”

Living on your own wavelength does not mean living without difficulty. An environment with no trigger at all does not charge you; it is empty. A person needs a manageable dose of their trigger: exactly the amount their purpose can cope with. What is radioactive is an overwhelming difficulty of a particular kind.

3Living on your own wavelength

The model leads to a practice: a set of skills that can be learned the way people learn hygiene or financial literacy.

  1. Know your “trigger and purpose” pair

    Without it, people put their slumps down to chance, to other people’s characters or to their own weakness. It helps to reconstruct the childhood scene as precisely as possible: which adult disrupted the relationship, whom you felt sorry for, and what, in the child’s view, should have been done.

  2. Keep track of your energy

    Notice after which people, conversations, places and activities you have more energy, and after which you have less. A rough test of work: if you have more energy after the working day than in the morning, that is a good sign the work fits your purpose.

  3. Choose your environment

    Move towards places where your purpose is needed: people, tasks and communities that need exactly your specific feature. Avoid radioactive situations, or at least do not get into them often.

  4. Interrupt

    Leave a situation quickly once it starts to drain you: end the conversation, change the subject, postpone the decision. This is ordinary hygiene.

  5. Compensate

    Energy is one pool: what you lose in one setting you can make up in another. If a radioactive loved one cannot be avoided, you can steer clear of the topics that set things off, meet when you have plenty of energy yourself, and recharge in other company. Concessions then become respect for their own path to happiness.

  6. Recharge deliberately

    Do not wait for life to offer a chance to use your purpose; create such situations regularly, in a dose that charges rather than exhausts. Otherwise a drama tends to arrive by itself, where you least expect it.

  7. Understand others

    Behaviour that hurts is most often explained by someone else’s program: a person going towards their happiness in their own way. Understanding does not remove the need for distance, but it removes resentment. Many conflicts begin with projecting one’s own sense of “good and bad” onto someone else.

  8. Protect the family with distance

    Parents are the prototype of their children’s program, so close together they are often radioactive for each other. Adult children and parents are often better off with some distance: at a distance, closeness remains instead of friction.

In my observation, people who have mastered these skills recover from hard situations much faster. I have seen it in myself: from my school years on, a difficult episode could knock me off balance for a month; now it takes about a day.

4Why this is an answer for a world without work

Work was a crude intermediary

For some, work happened to match their purpose: they were absorbed by it and did not tire. For others it was a radioactive environment where they lost energy for years. Most people received from work what Jahoda described (structure, company, a common cause) and paid for it with a mismatch. When work goes, the intermediary goes too. This world already exists in miniature: retirees have an income, yet many lose what work provided.7 What remains is the direct question: where, and with whom, does my purpose work?

Purpose cannot be automated

Machines will take over professional skills: calculation, analysis, production. Purpose is a person’s way of engaging with people and the world: to figure things out, to explain, to bring order, to support, to protect, to stand one’s ground. A machine will deliver the result faster; the value of purpose is that the person comes alive doing it.

The obvious objection is Bostrom’s: if machines do everything better, nobody needs what I do. My answer is that the triggers in this model are not economic. They are what people do to each other: shouting, withdrawing, leaving a mess, losing heart. Machines will not remove these. Dario Amodei makes a related point: a task does not lose its meaning because an AI can do it better.3 Whether people will still turn to each other rather than to machines is an open question that the model does not settle.

People will not necessarily go idle

A common fear is that without work people will sink into passive consumption, and some already do. The model suggests a condition. People who do not know their pair tend to drift. People who know it tend to move towards others, because for most people purpose is realised through other people: in communities, clubs, joint projects, mentoring, research, creative work. The task for society is to help people find their communities.

What society would need, if the tests in section 6 hold up

  • Purpose literacy. Explaining to adults how their strengths and vulnerabilities work. This is basic self-knowledge, not therapy.
  • Infrastructure for communities. Places and platforms where people gather not by profession but by the kind of activity that feeds them.
  • Environment matching. Services that help people find others, tasks and communities where their purpose is needed. A structured interview conducted by AI could make an assessment available to millions; such data are sensitive and would need strict protection. Matching by mutual “charge” could become for a world without work what the labour exchange was for the industrial world.
  • Research. Testing the model on large samples and refining it.

Income and meaning are two halves of one solution. Income removes the fear of survival. Meaning arises where a person applies what makes them who they are. A policy that provides the first without the second risks a society in a permanent “Sunday neurosis”.

5Going deeper

There is also a second path, for the few. If a person studies their program long and honestly, they may see that the childhood scene rested on a mistaken premise: the child decided the world had split because the parents quarrelled, whereas the unconditional joy of childhood faded simply because the child grew up and began to separate themselves from the world. Understanding this mistake, together with practices of attention and contemplation, can loosen the program’s grip: the person no longer feels compelled to prove, to fix, to fight. The skills remain, the inner compulsion goes, and a great deal of energy is freed for creative work and inquiry. Contemplative traditions, Buddhism among them, have long described this path; what I add is its link to the relationship program.

This is a hard path that takes many years of inner work; in my experience, few go all the way. For most people something else is enough, and valuable: living on their own wavelength.

6Limits of the model and how to test it

What the model is not

This is not a medical or clinical theory, and I make no claims about physical or mental health. It does not replace psychotherapy, treatment or medical care. None of it applies to situations of violence or abuse, where safety comes first.

Where it comes from and where it differs

Several older ideas are close to this model, and I owe a debt to them.

Known ideaWhat overlapsWhere the model differs
Life script, Eric Berne18A life plan decided in childhood under the parents’ influenceThe script is reduced to one “trigger and purpose” pair and used to predict gains and losses of energy
Individual psychology, Alfred Adler19A style of life formed in early childhood; compensationThe compensation is tied to a specific conflict between the adults
The gifted child, Alice Miller14Empathy as a child’s adaptation to a parentTreated as one case of a general mechanism that applies to many traits

Self-determination theory, Deci and Ryan21Well-being depends on an environment that supports basic needsThe route to competence and relatedness is person-specific

How to test it

I propose an open research programme:

  1. An open protocolPublish the interview questions, the coding rules and a list of purposes and triggers, so that others can make the assessment without me.
  2. ReliabilityTwo trained assessors independently determine the purpose and trigger of the same person. How closely do their conclusions agree?
  3. RecognitionA person is shown their own profile and three others. Can they pick out their own better than chance? This guards against the Barnum effect, people’s tendency to accept any plausible description of themselves.
  4. Predictive powerThe profile predicts in advance where a person will lose and gain energy; for several weeks the person then records their state during the day. Does the profile predict better than the person’s own guesses or a standard personality test?
  5. InterventionA randomised, preregistered trial run by independent researchers: people who learn their profile and practise living on their own wavelength are compared with people doing a standard strengths exercise, on life satisfaction, meaning in life and well-being after 3, 6 and 12 months.
  6. A pilot with basic income recipientsThree groups: money only; money and help finding communities; the same plus an assessment. Do sense of meaning, activity and well-being differ between them?

Agreement with a profile is not treated as confirmation, and disagreement is treated as data, not as resistance. I will consider the model refuted if trained assessors agree no better than they would on unrelated people, if people cannot tell their own profile from decoys, or if the profile predicts daily gains and losses of energy no better than people’s own guesses.

A first check

I have run a preliminary check of the first part of this programme, reliability and the link between the childhood scene and adult life, on 24 recorded consultations chosen at random. In each, the person’s account of their childhood and the account of their adult life were separated from each other and from my conclusions. Independent raters, who saw neither my conclusions nor the other part of the account, identified the pair using the written protocol. Two judges, AI models from different families, scored the similarity of the formulations blind; they agreed with each other on 87% of scores. Each real pair was compared with decoys, formulations taken from other people.

  • Reliability. Given the protocol and only the childhood account, a rater arrives at my trigger almost every time (at least a partial match in 96% of cases) and at the purpose in 79% of cases, far more often than with decoys.
  • Link to adult life. This is the part old recordings test least well. In a consultation I ask people not to describe their adult life in advance, “no spoilers”, and describe it myself from the childhood scene. So the recordings contain little of people’s own account of their adult life, and everything said after my description was excluded from the check. Even with this material, the kind of person who throws someone off balance in adult life matches the childhood scene more often than random pairs do. To test what gives a person energy, there was not enough material.

This is a preliminary check. The recordings were made for consultations, not for research, and they include my leading questions. The raters are AI models rather than trained people. The check does not prove the model, nor does it refute it by the criteria above.

In consultations people usually recognise themselves in the description of their adult life that I give from the childhood scene, sometimes with surprise. But, as stated above, agreement does not count as confirmation. So the next step is this: before the consultation the person fills in a short questionnaire about their adult life, I do not read it, and afterwards independent raters compare my description with that person’s questionnaire and with other people’s.

7An invitation

The question of meaning after work is being asked today by the people who build the technology and by those who study its effects. I believe it has an answer, and the answer begins with the person: with how they are made, and where they live on their own wavelength. The state and technology can only help.

I would like to invite into this conversation:

  • researchers of the future of work, psychologists and sociologists: I am looking for a co-investigator for a preregistered study on recorded consultations;
  • the builders of AI, so that technology helps people find their environments, rather than only replacing their labour;
  • those who design social policy, so that unconditional income comes with a real chance to find meaning;
  • everyone who already feels they are living someone else’s life.

About the author

I graduated from the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology (MIPT) and spent sixteen years as a systems engineer and software developer in large companies and banks. Today I work as an independent software developer.

Alongside this, I study how childhood experience shapes an adult’s strengths and vulnerabilities. I have conducted more than 150 personal consultations, host an open meeting once a month, and run the weekly sessions of the “On Your Own Wavelength” club, where participants learn the approach together and apply it in their own lives. The model described above grew out of this practice.

Another source of the model is contemplative practice. For many years I have practised meditation and studied Tibetan Buddhism; during several trips to India I studied with Tibetan teachers of several schools, including the Dalai Lama. This experience shows most in the path described in section 5.

Disclosure: my consultations and the club are paid. Consultations are recorded with clients’ consent, and the examples in this essay are anonymised. That is one more reason I want others to test the model. I am not a therapist or a physician; this essay offers a hypothesis to be tested, not a clinical method.
